# What Are Stock Perpetual Futures?

Stock perpetual futures are derivatives designed to give traders price exposure to an equity, ETF, or stock-market index without owning the underlying share. Unlike traditional futures, perpetual contracts do not have a fixed expiry date.

This guide explains how these contracts work, what they do—and do not—represent, and which risks traders should understand before using leverage.

## 1. Perpetual Futures Are Price-Exposure Instruments

A stock perpetual future tracks a reference price for an underlying market, such as an individual equity or an index. It is a derivative contract, not a share certificate.

Holding a perpetual position generally does not provide shareholder rights. It does not normally include voting rights, dividend ownership, or a claim on the issuer's assets. Traders should read the product specification of the venue they use and understand the distinction between market exposure and ownership.

## 2. Why Perpetual Contracts Do Not Expire

Conventional futures settle or expire on a scheduled date. Perpetual futures remain open until the trader closes the position or the position is liquidated.

Because there is no expiry, trading venues use a funding mechanism to help keep the contract price close to its reference market. Funding payments can add to the cost of holding a position, especially during extended periods when market sentiment is strongly one-sided.

## 3. Key Terms to Understand

### Mark price

The mark price is commonly used to calculate unrealized profit and loss and to help determine liquidation thresholds. It may differ from the most recent trade price.

### Funding rate

Funding is a periodic payment exchanged between long and short positions. It is not the same as a trading fee. A positive or negative funding rate can materially affect the result of a position held over time.

### Leverage and margin

Leverage amplifies both gains and losses. A small move in the reference price can have a large effect on the margin supporting a position. Isolated margin can limit the margin assigned to one position, but it does not remove the risk of losing that assigned margin.

## 4. Stock Perpetuals Versus Owning Shares

| Topic | Stock perpetual future | Owning a share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure | Tracks a reference price | Direct equity ownership |
| Expiry | Typically no fixed expiry | No contract expiry |
| Dividends | Product-specific adjustment, if any | Subject to issuer distributions |
| Voting rights | Usually none | May apply to eligible shareholders |
| Leverage | Often available | Depends on brokerage margin rules |
| Liquidation | Possible when margin is insufficient | Not a futures liquidation mechanism |

## 5. A Practical Risk Checklist

Before opening a position, consider:

- Whether the contract tracks the expected reference market.
- The funding schedule and recent funding-rate conditions.
- The difference between last price and mark price.
- Maximum leverage, maintenance margin, and liquidation rules.
- Market hours and the treatment of price discovery when the reference market is closed.
- Liquidity, spread, and the venue's contract specifications.
- Whether you can afford to lose the margin allocated to the trade.
